## Reasoning
Q_means is the strongest signal: the paper explicitly documents that the *existing* theory for this old, well-documented phenomenon fails on quantitative grounds for the very substance (water) the phenomenon is observed in, and the paper's contribution is a *new* model. That is the "old problem, new capability" configuration this batch has already surfaced twice (W2019385975, W2040371569) — and the codebook's own enabler vocabulary explicitly includes "new-theory-or-formalism" as a means-first capability type, supporting treating a new analytical model the same way as a new instrument or assay. Q_problem reaches only "partial": the phenomenon and the specific failure of prior theory are old and well-documented, but the ingredient that actually closes the gap (the new curvature-based model) is new-here, not pre-existing. Q_idea reaches "partial" rather than "yes": the abstract's framing ("we demonstrate how...") could be read as a design-organizing proposition, but it functions in the text as the paper's conclusion drawn from combined measurement and modeling, not a claim staked out before the work. Q_accretion is "no." Q_means is the only clean "yes," so it is the assignment regardless of the reversed order's tie-breaking priority.

## Alternative reading
Idea-first is the more interesting alternative here than usual: if the introduction had opened by asserting "the tip angle should be governed by front curvature via heat transfer, not by the density-ratio criterion of prior theory" as a stated premise before any new measurements were taken, this would be a strong idea-first case (a proposition organizing a design built to test it). The actual text places that claim as the outcome of "we demonstrate," which is why I did not promote it past "partial." Problem-first remains the other live alternative, on the same reasoning as the two other "old problem, new capability" cases in this batch.
