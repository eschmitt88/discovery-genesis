## Reasoning
Q_problem is the clear "yes": the specific gap (this taxon's mitogenome had not been sequenced) is named directly, and it is closed with a pipeline explicitly described as producing results "similar with related reports before" — i.e., an unmodified, already-standard method. Q_means is "no" for the same reason: nothing about the sequencing, annotation, or phylogenetic method is new to the authors or the field. Q_idea is "no" — no proposition organizes the design. Q_accretion required genuine consideration: this genre (single-species mitogenome deposit papers) is so formulaic across the literature that it has real "fill in the numbers" character, and I rated it "partial" rather than "no" for that reason — but the contribution is original sequencing of a new specimen, not an aggregation of already-published data, so it falls short of the accretion test as written. With problem-first a clean "yes" against accretion's "partial," and accretion weighted last under the reversed order regardless, problem-first is the assignment.

## Alternative reading
Accretion is the more interesting alternative than usual for this genre, precisely because of how repetitive and standardized these single-species mitogenome papers are across the literature (evident from the near-identical phrasing shared with the cited prior papers). If this were explicitly a multi-species compilation or database submission paper rather than one new specimen's original sequencing, I would code it accretion instead.

## Notes for the codebook
This is a recognizable, high-volume genre (single-species mitogenome + phylogenetic-placement papers) that reads as formulaic gap-filling:first-measurement but sits close to the accretion boundary because of its templated form — worth a codebook note distinguishing "original data collection in a highly standardized report format" (problem-first) from "aggregation of others' already-published data into a standardized report format" (accretion), since the two can look superficially similar.
