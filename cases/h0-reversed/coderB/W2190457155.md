## Reasoning
Q_problem carries the card: the introduction states, almost as a textbook problem-first paragraph, what is known (hypothermia raises productivity and chaperone levels), what is specifically unknown (the post-translational/ERAD mechanism, and whether it's temperature or growth rate driving it), and then closes that gap using tools cited from the literature (chemostat decoupling from ref. [20]; standard ERAD-pathway inhibitors). Q_means is "no" under the v1.1 rule — every tool used is cited as pre-existing, none introduced or newly adapted in this paper. Q_idea is "no": the framing throughout is "the effect... was investigated," an open research question rather than a proposition risked in advance. Q_accretion is trivially "no." With only one clear "yes," the reversed order changes nothing.

## Alternative reading
Means-first would require treating the chemostat-based decoupling design itself as a new capability. It is not — it is explicitly attributed to a specific prior study (ref. [20]) as the reason chemostat culture was chosen at all. The authors' own framing treats the *design logic* (decouple two confounded variables) as borrowed, and only the *system it's applied to* (rht-PA ERAD in CHO cells) as new — which is the definition of problem-first, not means-first.

## Notes for the codebook
A useful test of the means-first tell about self-citation counts (dossier support note, 2026-08-24): here the enabling method is cited to an *external* group's paper, not the authors' own prior work, yet it still reads as problem-first because the capability itself (chemostat culture) is old and field-standard, not novel even to that external group. The self-citation heuristic should probably be read as "≥4 self-citations of the same method → problem-first" but its absence does not automatically imply means-first; field-standard techniques cited to others still default to problem-first if the paper's contribution is the question, not the tool.
