## Reasoning
Idea-first, heard first, holds up well here: the paper diagnoses a precise physical failure mechanism in the prior paradigm and states, as a design rationale preceding the results, a specific structural proposition (unify filter and nonlinear roles to suppress mode-beating noise) that the whole experiment is built to confirm — this is close to the textbook idea-first pattern of "claim organizes design." Means-first is a genuine competitor since the enabling hardware (the high-Q CMOS-compatible microresonator) is what makes the demonstration possible, but that hardware is the authors' own previously established platform (self-cited to their 2009 papers on CMOS-compatible microring oscillators), which under v1.1 argues against calling it a "new" capability — it is the conceptual reconfiguration of its role, not its introduction, that is new. Problem-first is weaker: while achieving stable high-repetition mode-locking is a known engineering goal, the paper's own framing centers a stated mechanistic proposition rather than simply combining existing ingredients to close a generic goal.

## Alternative reading
Means-first is recorded as the tie. A coder weighting the hardware capability more heavily than the conceptual reframing would reasonably call this means-first — the high-Q microresonator is genuinely central to the physical demonstration, even if it predates this specific paper in the authors' own hands.

## Notes for the codebook
A useful physics-domain example of the idea-first/means-first boundary when an established piece of hardware is given a new *conceptual role* (filter+nonlinear-medium unification) rather than being newly invented — worth a worked example distinguishing "new capability" (means-first) from "existing capability given a new structural role, driven by an explicit mechanistic proposition" (idea-first).
