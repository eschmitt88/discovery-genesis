## Reasoning
Idea-first fails outright: there is no proposition, only two sequential engineering objectives motivated by named gaps in prior work on this specific enzyme. Means-first is tempting given the paper's own claim that the SLP-encapsulation approach is "reported for first time in this study," but on inspection this is a first *application* of an already-established technology (solid lipid particles via hot lipid emulsion, previously validated for itraconazole, primaquine, and curcumin) to a new target (this particular enzyme) — not the introduction of a new capability. That is the codebook's gap-filling:next-target pattern applied to formulation science. Problem-first is the better fit: named limitations in the existing state of the art (no overexpression system for this gene; existing stabilization methods too complex) are closed by combining pre-existing techniques.

## Alternative reading
A coder emphasizing the "reported for first time in this study" language could reasonably lean means-first, treating the SLP-L-asparaginase combination itself as a new capability. I judged the underlying formulation technology's prior validation on other drugs as decisive against that reading.

## Notes for the codebook
A pharmaceutical-formulation example of gap-filling:next-target where the "target" is a specific therapeutic protein rather than a species, population, or region — the established technology (SLP encapsulation) is transplanted from small-molecule drug delivery to a protein-drug context for the first time. Worth noting alongside the microsphere/aptamer paper (W2729223875) as a second formulation-science instance of this pattern.
