## Reasoning
Idea-first fails: there is no proposition here, only an engineering combination motivated by named trade-offs among existing reconfiguration technologies. Means-first also fails decisively under the v1.1 self-citation rule — the liquid-crystal tuning capability that could otherwise look like a "new capability enabling the finding" is in fact the authors' own well-established platform, reused here (not introduced) in combination with a different, also pre-existing, architecture (folded reflectarray). This is recombination-as-move with problem-first as the genesis: the specific engineering gap (a reconfigurable antenna without feed blockage, without the cost/complexity of MEMS/diodes) is closed by combining two already-existing pieces from adjacent literatures within the same field.

## Alternative reading
One could argue means-first if the "capability" is defined narrowly as *this particular combination* (LC + folded architecture) rather than either ingredient individually — but the codebook's means-first test is about a capability whose *availability* is the reason the finding exists, and here both capabilities were already available to the authors well before this paper; what is new is the combination (recombination), not an underlying capability.

## Notes for the codebook
This is a useful example of "recombination as move, but problem-first as genesis" — worth flagging because it is easy to conflate "the combination is new" with "means-first," when the self-citation evidence shows neither component technology is new to the authors. A worked example distinguishing "novel combination of two pre-existing platforms" (problem-first) from "one genuinely new capability enabling a finding" (means-first) would help.
