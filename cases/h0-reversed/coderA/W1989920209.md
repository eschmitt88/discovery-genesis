## Reasoning
Idea-first, heard first, is well supported: the paper diagnoses a precise, specific contradiction in the existing literature (predicted vs. actual density-ratio threshold) and then states its own mechanistic explanation as a claim preceding the data — this is close to the textbook idea-first pattern. Means-first fails clearly since the experimental apparatus is standard. Problem-first is a real but weaker alternative: the underlying question (what causes the tip singularity) is itself old and specific, and one could read the heat-transfer model as simply the correct combination of pre-existing ingredients (solidification theory, heat-transfer geometry) rather than a genuinely new proposition — hence the recorded tie.

## Alternative reading
Problem-first is the closest competitor and a coder emphasizing that heat-transfer theory and solidification-front modeling are themselves pre-existing tools (not new capabilities) would reasonably land there instead — the "ingredient" (heat-transfer-driven front geometry) was available in principle before this paper, and the contribution can be read as correctly identifying which existing ingredient resolves a known puzzle.

## Notes for the codebook
A second physics example (alongside the microcavity laser paper) of the idea-first/problem-first boundary turning on whether a paper's central move is best read as "a new mechanistic proposition, tested" versus "an old puzzle closed by correctly selecting among pre-existing ingredients." Both readings are defensible; the codebook may want a rule of thumb (e.g., does the paper state its explanatory claim *before* the results, as a falsifiable prediction, rather than presenting it as the conclusion of induction from data?).
