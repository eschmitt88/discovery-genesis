## Reasoning
Idea-first, heard first under the reversed order, does not hold: there is no stated proposition, only a measurement objective ("we analyze spontaneous mutations..."). The competition is between means-first and problem-first. I favor means-first because the title and framing center the *method* (whole-genome sequencing) as what makes the paper's specific findings (strand-specific transition bias, sequence-context hotspots) possible — these are findings that the pre-existing "ingredients" (fluctuation tests, single reporter loci) structurally could not have produced, since they only sample a tiny biased fraction of the genome. That is the means-first signature: capability → previously-invisible observation → claim.

## Alternative reading
Problem-first is plausible too: the question "what is the E. coli mutation rate and spectrum" is decades old (references back to 1952, 1991 fluctuation-rate work), and WGS by 2012 was not brand-new to the field generally (established since ~2005-2008), so one could argue an "existing, if recently improved, resource" was simply pointed at a long-standing question. I coded genesis_tie: problem-first to record this.

## Notes for the codebook
Distinguishing "capability new to the field broadly" from "capability new to this specific research question/subfield" is doing real work here — WGS was not new to genomics in 2012, but it was new to fine-grained spontaneous-mutation-spectrum work, which had been dominated by reporter-gene methods. The codebook's means-first test doesn't cleanly specify which scope ("field" = genomics broadly, or "field" = the mutation-rate subfield) should govern; recommend clarifying.
