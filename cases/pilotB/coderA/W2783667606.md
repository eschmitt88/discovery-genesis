## Reasoning
Accretion is clearly wrong (a mechanistic causal claim, not an aggregate). Means-first doesn't fit: no new instrument or assay is introduced — the CIN-suppression genetics and the cGAS-STING pathway both already existed in the literature (2013-2015 refs), and it is the stated open causal question ("bystander or driver?") that motivates the specific experimental design, not a new capability stumbled into. That makes problem-first the correct call under the decision order. The move is best read as unification: CIN/aneuploidy biology and innate-immune DNA sensing are genuinely separate research programs (cancer genetics vs. immunology) that this paper shows to be one mechanism in the metastasis context — stronger than "recombination" because the two source literatures have different primary fields.

## Alternative reading
Means-first is the plausible runner-up if one reads the paper as "we had CIN-suppression tools in hand from our prior work, so we asked what else they could reveal" — capability-driven exploration rather than question-driven. I ranked problem-first higher because the explicit "remains unclear whether..." framing in the opening sentence is a textbook problem-first marker per the codebook.

## Notes for the codebook
Good example of the unification/recombination boundary working as intended — cGAS-STING (immunology) and CIN (cancer cell-division genetics) are different primary fields, so unification (or at minimum transfer-flavoured unification) reads more accurately here than recombination.
