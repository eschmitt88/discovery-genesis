## Reasoning
The paper opens as a compliance audit (Sherwood 2001 is the direct model, primary care -> secondary care) which would tempt an accretion call, but the paper's own abstract and discussion foreground a genuinely novel, unpredicted empirical relationship (jaundice/survival) that could not have been written with only numbers left blank. The ingredient that resolves it -- the retrospective admissions cohort -- pre-existed as routine clinical data; nothing new was invented to measure it. That combination (named open question + pre-existing data) is the definition of problem-first. Evidence that would change my mind: an introduction stating this analysis was pre-specified as a hypothesis test from the outset (would strengthen idea-first), or evidence the audit's compliance-reporting purpose was the paper's real point (would push toward accretion).

## Alternative reading
Accretion is plausible for the compliance-audit half of the paper (a service evaluation whose conclusion's form -- "we were/were not compliant" -- could be written before the numbers). I judged the jaundice/survival finding as the paper's real center of gravity (it is what both abstract and discussion dwell on), which tips the call to problem-first.
