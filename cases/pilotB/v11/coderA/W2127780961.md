## Reasoning
This is a straightforward "same question, much larger/longer sample drawn from routine practice" case: the specific clinical question (safety of extended screening intervals with cotesting) was already being asked and partly answered by randomized trials; this paper's contribution is size and real-world generalizability, using data that already existed as part of Kaiser Permanente's clinical operations rather than a purpose-built instrument. Evidence that would change my mind: full text showing the statistical/risk-modeling approach used (e.g., cumulative-incidence estimation methods) was itself a novel methodological contribution rather than standard survival analysis, which would push toward means-first.

## Alternative reading
Accretion is a weak alternative if this is read as primarily a large descriptive report of cancer incidence by cotest result (a "database"-style contribution). I judged problem-first over accretion because the abstract frames a specific policy question (is extending intervals safe; does cotesting improve identification of high-risk women) rather than simply reporting aggregate statistics.

## Notes for the codebook
Good example of gap-filling:bigger-n where the "bigger sample" is not a purpose-collected larger trial but a repurposed operational/clinical dataset -- worth distinguishing from bigger-n cases built from planned prospective cohorts, since the enabler (existing-resource-repurposed vs new-dataset-or-resource) differs even when the move label is the same.
