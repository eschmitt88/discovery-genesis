## Reasoning
The introduction explicitly lays out a two-part, purely descriptive/confirmatory agenda (establish the trend, then test known correlates), and every substantive risk factor tested (peer smoking, parental monitoring, truancy, perceived access/risk, family structure) already appears in the cited literature from the 1990s-2000s. The paper's genuine news — a closing gender gap and a 28-point prevalence decline — is a finding about magnitude/direction, not a new causal idea; the regression analysis is a standard, off-the-shelf template applied to newly available data (the 2015 wave). This is squarely accretion: "a large trial of an established question" using a maturing longitudinal dataset. Evidence that would change my mind: a full-text passage proposing a genuinely new mechanism for the closing gender gap (e.g., a specific new causal hypothesis tested with a novel design) rather than testing the same established covariate list.

## Alternative reading
Problem-first is a plausible second reading: the closing gender gap is itself a fairly recent (within-dataset) empirical observation, and one could argue the newly matured dataset (an "ingredient" less than 3 years old relative to publication) forced this specific analysis. I rate it below accretion because the analytic approach applied to that new ingredient is entirely conventional (standard logistic regression on a well-worn covariate list), which is closer to "large trial of an established question" than to a problem meeting a genuinely novel technique.
