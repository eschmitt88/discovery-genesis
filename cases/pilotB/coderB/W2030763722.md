## Reasoning
Without abstract or full text, the case rests on what the reference list and title imply. MLPA itself (2002) and its specific application to 22q11.2 deletion diagnosis (2006, 2007) were both already established by the time of this paper; the other necessary ingredient — routinely collected neonatal dried blood spots, banked since the PKU-screening era (1968-1999 refs) — is far older still. The paper's apparent contribution is combining these two already-existing pieces to unlock a new use case (screening from archived spots rather than fresh samples), which is the defining problem-first pattern: an old, standing problem (detecting this syndrome broadly / at scale) meets a relatively recent, already-available ingredient (validated MLPA-22q11 assay) with no need for anything conceptually new. Evidence that would change my mind: full text revealing a genuinely new technical adaptation (e.g., overcoming DNA degradation specific to dried blood spots) that was non-trivial, which would push this toward means-first (the technical workaround as the real discovery).

## Alternative reading
Accretion is a plausible alternative: this could be read as one more incremental technical-validation study (yet another sample type demonstrated to work with an existing assay), with no real "move" beyond routine method extension. I lean problem-first over accretion because the shift from cultured-cell/fresh-blood diagnostics to a dried-blood-spot screening use case does open a genuinely new application (population-level retrospective screening) rather than just reproducing the same use case on new material — but I can't confirm this distinction without the full text.
