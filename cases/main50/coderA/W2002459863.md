## Reasoning
The clearest genesis signal here is authorial: the pan-genome concept and its two standard estimation models are cited to a 2005 paper by co-author Hervé Tettelin studying a different species (S. agalactiae), and this paper applies the same framework to S. pneumoniae — precisely the codebook's "next species" worked example for problem-first / gap-filling:next-target. `recombination` is kept as a second label because the study's central finding (extensive genetic exchange across species boundaries) required joining S. pneumoniae data with related Mitis-group species datasets within the same field, which is a distinct combinatorial act beyond simply re-running the pan-genome method on a new species alone; removing it would understate that cross-species integration.

## Alternative reading
means-first is a plausible alternative if the specific enabling factor is treated as the newly assembled 44-genome, multi-species dataset itself (a scale/resource capability rather than a target extension) — by 2010, however, multi-strain bacterial sequencing was already routine (the cited 2005 pan-genome paper itself used multiple genomes), so I read the dataset as an application of established sequencing capacity rather than a new-to-the-field capability.

## Notes for the codebook
Confirms the codebook's own "next species" worked example almost exactly, but also shows a real wrinkle: the paper isn't purely single-species next-target, it also integrates data across species boundaries (recombination) as part of the same move. A combined next-target+recombination worked example (as here) may be useful alongside the pure single-species case.
