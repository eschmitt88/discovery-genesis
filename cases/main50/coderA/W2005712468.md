## Reasoning
The introduction states the problem directly and specifically ("It is unknown why sea turtles ingest plastic") and lays out three explicit aims. The central methodological move — applying a resource selection function, "a method traditionally used for habitat and food selection," to debris ingestion — is a same-field repurposing of an established ecological/statistical tool, not a new instrument or dataset type. Both the necropsy protocol and the beach-survey protocol are standard field-ecology methods. This is problem-first: the specific question (why/how do turtles select debris) is what's new, and the RSF machinery that answers it already existed in the authors' broader field (habitat-selection ecology). gap-filling:next-target captures this well since it is an established-method-on-new-target case, though the target here is a question rather than a species/population; recombination was considered but rejected because RSF-for-selection and turtle-diet-ecology are not two separate literatures being unified — RSF is squarely within the same wildlife-ecology methods toolkit being pointed at a new dependent variable.

## Alternative reading
Means-first is a weaker but not absurd alternative if using RSF for debris (rather than habitat/prey) selection is itself considered a genuinely new modeling capability for the marine-debris-ingestion literature specifically (as opposed to ecology broadly). I judged this a repurposing rather than a new capability because RSF's mechanics are unchanged; only the application domain shifts.

## Notes for the codebook
Good example of the transfer/recombination/gap-filling boundary: importing a standard method from one sub-question (habitat selection) to a sibling sub-question (debris selection) within the same field and same broader toolkit is gap-filling:next-target, not transfer (no field-boundary crossing) and arguably not recombination either (nothing genuinely separate is being joined).
