## Reasoning
The abstract's findings (strand-specific transition biases, sequence-context hotspots, indel rates relative to substitutions) require genome-wide, base-resolution data — the kind WGS provides. But WGS-based mutation-spectrum studies were already published in yeast (2008) and human (2010), both cited and both predating this paper by 2-4 years; the reference list's E. coli-specific precedents (1987, 2003) use marker-gene sequencing, not WGS. That pattern — an established capability (WGS mutation-accumulation analysis), not new to the field, applied to the next organism — is problem-first, `gap-filling:next-target`.

## Alternative reading
Means-first is plausible if this is in fact the first WGS application to *bacterial* mutation accumulation specifically (as opposed to eukaryotic yeast/human), which would make bacteria itself a meaningfully new regime for the method (different repair systems, genome size, replication mode) rather than a routine target swap. Full text discussing whether earlier bacterial WGS mutation studies existed would resolve this; abstract alone cannot, hence medium confidence.

## Notes for the codebook
A genuinely useful worked example for `next-target`: the "target" here is an organism/domain (bacteria vs. eukaryotes) rather than a species within one domain, and the field-level (not author-level) capability precedent (yeast, human) is what makes the call, since this dossier shows no obvious self-citation chain for the authors' own prior WGS work.
