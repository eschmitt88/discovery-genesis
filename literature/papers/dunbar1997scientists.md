# How scientists think: On-line creativity and conceptual change in science

## TL;DR

In-vivo videotaped observation of four molecular-biology labs (19
scientists, one year, weekly lab meetings) finds that discoveries mostly
arise from *unexpected findings* noticed and argued over in real time,
reasoned about via *local, within-domain analogies* — not from a
stated idea pursued to confirmation. Distant, cross-domain analogy (the
textbook picture of scientific creativity) is rare.

## Claims

- Most creative reasoning in these labs happens at the weekly lab
  meeting, not in individual reflection — it is social/distributed, not
  solitary.
- Unexpected findings are common and are *not* explained away: the group
  spends more reasoning effort and interaction on unexpected than on
  expected findings, and pursues them into new hypotheses.
- Analogical reasoning is dominated by *near* analogies (within the same
  organism or a related organism), not the distant, gestalt-restructuring
  analogies (Rutherford/solar-system-style) that the creativity
  literature usually foregrounds.
- One documented case (the "Anatomy of a Conceptual Change" episode) has
  a discovery occurring live, on tape, during a lab meeting — direct
  evidence against the idea that the paper's introduction narrates how
  the idea actually arose.

## Methods

- Six candidate labs identified via consultation (including a Nobel
  laureate) and literature search; four selected. One year embedded,
  weekly lab-meeting audio/video, pre- and post-meeting interviews with
  presenters and PIs (pre-present-post design), plus grant proposals,
  paper drafts, and impromptu meetings as supplementary sources.
- Coded by two independent transcribers/coders at the level of the
  statement/utterance (protocol-analysis style); reliability checked by
  independent coders.
- Findings coded as "expected" / "unexpected" (inconsistent with the
  stated hypothesis) / "exploratory" (no stated hypothesis to violate);
  analogies coded by range (within-organism / other-organism /
  non-biological) and by goal (formulate hypothesis / design experiment /
  explain / fix experiment).

## Results

- Across 4 meetings / 6 experiments / 70 conditions: 22 expected, 18
  unexpected, 30 exploratory findings — unexpected findings are common,
  not an edge case.
- Group attention to unexpected vs. expected findings: 176 interactions
  on unexpected findings vs. 23 on expected findings; more reasoning
  blocks generated per unexpected finding, and little tendency to
  explain them away.
- 99 analogies across 16 meetings: 40 within-organism, 57 other-organism,
  only 2 non-biological — almost all analogical reasoning is local, not
  the "distant domain" transfer that creativity folklore emphasizes.
  Analogies were most often used to *explain* (45) rather than to
  formulate a hypothesis (23).

## Critique / open questions

- Four labs, one field (molecular biology), one era (1990s) — external
  validity to other STEM fields and to solo/theoretical work (vs. large
  experimental groups) is untested by this study alone.
- "Unexpected finding → reinterpreted in real time" is the means-first
  signature this project's coding protocol needs worked examples of; the
  paper does not itself code a genesis_model taxonomy, so the mapping to
  this project's four models is this project's contribution, not
  Dunbar's.

## Trust signals

- **Credibility:** 4 — McGill-based cognitive scientist, published as an
  APA-edited academic volume chapter (editorial peer review, not journal
  peer review), 484 citations; methodologically unusual (no other
  study has replicated the same in-vivo lab-meeting design at this
  scale) so treated as strong but singular evidence.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance:** 5 — the primary empirical grounding for means-first
  genesis (`genesis-models`) and for treating "the idea was recognized
  during ongoing work" as a real, common pattern rather than a rare
  exception; the expected/unexpected/exploratory coding scheme and the
  near-vs-distant analogy distinction are directly reusable in the
  `move-taxonomy` codebook (e.g., "anomaly" and "analogy-transfer" as
  distinct moves, with a *distance* sub-feature).
